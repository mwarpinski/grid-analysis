Power markets assign prices to each node in a directed graph that makes up the power grid. The edges of this directed graph are transmission lines that all have limits on the amount of electricity flow they can handle. The prices on the nodes are called LMPs or Locational Marginal Pricing, because they represent the cost of supplying one more megawatt into the grid at a specific location. LMPs have 3 different components:


Energy: the actual cost of energy produced. This component should be about the same across every price node
Congestion: the modeled premium/discount at a specific location to encourage/discourage supply and demand at this location on the grid - This is what we care about
Loss: the energy loss that occurs in transmission to get energy to this location (somewhat negligible)


The price is determined by auctions in 2 different markets: the Day Ahead (DA) and Real Time (RT) markets. The Day Ahead market has generators (supply) and load entities (demand) make bids for all 24 hr segments of the day to create the cost of energy at each price node. The real time market has the same auction mechanism but split into 5 minute segments. I’m massively oversimplifying/glossing over this, but I mention the 2 markets because the way that people make money in this market is by buying energy in the DA market and selling in the RT market when the RT price ends up being higher (or vice versa).


Since the Congestion component is what drives price changes, this is the main focus of financial traders before making trades. They’ll want to identify nodes on the grid that are over- or under-modeling congestion in the DA market so they can buy or sell energy at specific locations.

A constraint shift factor represents how much the flow on a constrained transmission element will change (in percentage) for a 1 MW change in generation/load at a specific pricing node. When multiplied by the constraint's shadow price, it determines that constraint's contribution to the node's Marginal Congestion Component (MCC) of LMP.


ISOs publish the constraints along with their shadow price in both the DA and RT markets. They also publish the MCC for each time period. This makes calculating shift factors very simple when looking at a time period with only a single binding constraint. For example, download the two files linked in the top level bullets:

Shadow price is -802.703
You can interpret this shadow price’s large negative number as saying: on net, there’s too much energy being injected into the grid right now and it’s causing this constraint to bind. On net prices must go down to reduce power production on the export side of this constraint, or load must increase on the export side to keep the system in balance.

Powerflow modeling is how the ISOs calculate shift factors. When I ask Claude how ISOs calculate shift factors this is what I get in response:

Shift factors (also called Power Transfer Distribution Factors or PTDFs) are calculated by ISOs to determine how power flows through the transmission system when generation or load changes at different locations. Here's an explanation of how they are typically calculated:

Definition:
A shift factor represents the percentage of power that will flow on a specific transmission line when 1 MW is injected at a specific node and withdrawn at a reference bus
Values range from -1 to 1 (or -100% to 100%)
Negative values indicate power flows in the opposite direction of the defined positive flow
Calculation Method:
Uses DC power flow analysis (linearized approximation of AC power flow)
The basic formula is: Shift Factor = Change in Line Flow / Change in Transaction

Key Components:
Network topology (transmission line connections)
Line impedances
Location of injection/withdrawal points
Reference bus location

Process:
a) Create network model with:
Bus admittance matrix
Line parameters
System topology
b) Solve using:
Matrix operations
DC power flow equations
Linear sensitivity analysis
Uses:
Security constrained economic dispatch
Congestion management
Financial transmission rights (FTR) markets
Locational marginal pricing (LMP) calculations
